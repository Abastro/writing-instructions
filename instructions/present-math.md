# Mathematical Presentation

## Parameter

- **Intended audience**: What do they already know? What would they want to learn about the work, and in what terms would they recognize an advance?
- **Presentation task**: What to prepare or revise, the format, and the time available.

## Instructions

- Develop the argument from the main idea down to the details.
    State the mathematical goal in terms the audience can follow on first hearing. Give the simplest account of the strategy they can use to see where the talk is going.
    Before developing a part of the argument, show what it contributes to the whole.
- Let the audience’s next question guide the order within each part.
    If a natural approach reveals an obstacle, give the audience time to see where it fails. A small example can make the difficulty visible and later show how the new idea resolves it.
- Choose the mathematical details by asking what the audience should be able to explain afterward.
    In a research overview, this may be what a construction gains, what it gives up, and the idea that makes the trade possible.
    Give the decisive step enough time to explain why it works. When invoking a lemma or sketching a proof, say what you are taking as given and what follows from it.
    Preserve the hypotheses, quantifier order, and parameter dependencies of each claim you present.
- Introduce notation when the audience needs it.
    Keep the current goal and any definitions needed for a calculation visible together when possible. When returning to a symbol after an interval, remind the audience what it represents.
- Prefer a visual explanation when it makes the mathematics easier to follow at a glance.
    Choose a form that shows the idea directly. An annotated expression can locate the source of an error, a diagram can trace a dependency, and aligned figures can reveal what changes. Use short labels and leave the fuller explanation to the speaker.
- Give the audience a specific reason to inspect each display.
    Before a calculation, say what it should establish. Allow time to read the expression, then point out the term, cancellation, or bound that does the work.
- Use transitions to return from a detail to the main argument.
    After a proof step, say what has now been established and why it matters for the goal. At the end, return to the opening question and show how the central idea answers it.
- Check the talk from the audience’s position as it unfolds.
    At each major transition, can someone see why the next step matters? Are the facts and symbols needed to follow it still in view or fresh enough to recall?
    Inspect each slide at presentation size. If a label is hard to read, cut detail before shrinking the type.
